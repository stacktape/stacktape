This policy explains what data Stacktape s.r.o., company ID 53071930, Ľudovíta Fullu 3104/15, 841 05 Bratislava, Slovakia ("Stacktape", "we", "us" or "our") collects when you use the Stacktape CLI, the Stacktape Console, this website and the documentation, and what we do with it. The terms of use cover the rest of our relationship.

In short:

  • Without an account, the CLI sends only anonymous usage data. One environment variable turns it off.
  • The Console stores your account, your organization's deploy history and costs, and operational data: error events, alarm and check results, and security findings. Known kinds of sensitive text are masked before they leave your AWS account.
  • Application logs, build artifacts, package lists and secrets stay in your AWS account.

1. What the CLI sends

Without an account

The CLI sends anonymous usage data, so we can see which commands fail and fix them. The first time you run it, it tells you what it sends and how to turn it off, before it sends anything. It sends only:

  • the command name;
  • whether the command succeeded, and the error code if it failed;
  • the CLI version, operating system and CPU architecture;
  • whether it runs in CI;
  • a random installation ID.

It never sends project, stack, stage or AWS account names or IDs, file paths, configuration or error messages. Telemetry is off when STP_DISABLE_TELEMETRY=1 or DO_NOT_TRACK=1 is set, and always off in CI.

Without an account, the CLI does not call the Stacktape API. Besides telemetry, it checks for a newer CLI version, fetches short notices such as planned maintenance, and downloads Stacktape's public CloudFormation resource types from a public S3 bucket when a stack uses them.

stacktape init can analyze your repository with a coding agent you have installed, such as Claude Code or Codex. The agent runs on your machine and sends what it reads to its own AI provider, under your account with that provider. Stacktape does not receive your code.

When you are signed in

With a Stacktape account, the CLI sends usage statistics and reports of its own crashes:

  • After every command: the command name, the names of the options you used (not their values), how long it took, whether it succeeded, the error code if it failed, your operating system, locale, time zone and the CLI version.
  • From some features: summaries made of categories and counts, for example how far a stacktape init session got and which frameworks and resource types it found.
  • When the CLI crashes: the error type, message and stack trace, the command, your operating system, CPU architecture and whether it ran in CI. Errors in your configuration, code or AWS account are not reported this way.
  • Identity: a random ID of your machine. After stacktape login, events are linked to your Stacktape user and organization, and your email and name are attached to your user.

Before a crash report leaves your machine, the CLI masks tokens and other credentials, email and IP addresses, AWS account IDs and the user name in home-directory paths. Error messages can still contain other text, such as file or resource names. The CLI never sends configuration values, source code, environment variables or AWS credentials. The same two environment variables turn all of this off.

A signed-in CLI also records your deploys in the Console, as described in the next section.

2. What the Console stores

When you use the Console, or the CLI signed in to it, Stacktape stores the following in its own AWS account, in the eu-west-1 region (Ireland):

  • Your account: your name, email address and sign-in settings, and the organizations you belong to with your role in each.
  • Your organization: projects and stages, connected AWS accounts, deploy history (who deployed what, when and with what result), the audit log, API keys, and the GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket and Slack connections you set up.
  • Costs: the AWS costs of your Stacktape-managed stacks, read from your AWS billing data.
  • Error events: for each detected error, its message and type, the stack frames, the matching log line or the lines around it (4,000 characters at most), and where it happened.
  • Alarm and check results: alarm state changes, and the results of uptime checks: up or down, HTTP status, timings, a short failure reason and the certificate's expiry date. Response bodies are not sent.
  • Security findings: configuration findings from your deploys and the known vulnerabilities in your packages. Secret values are never sent.
  • Incidents built from these signals, and their AI briefings if your organization connects an AI key.
  • Billing: your plan, the billing email, customer name and currency, and the monthly charges. Paddle handles payment details, and we do not receive your card number.
  • Usage analytics: the Console pages you open and some actions you take, linked to your user (with your name and email) and organization, and reports of Console errors. Session recording is off.

Before error text leaves your AWS account, known kinds of sensitive data are masked: secrets and tokens, values under names such as password or token, credentials in URLs, email and IP addresses, card numbers and home-directory paths. The Console masks them again on arrival. Names, street addresses and phone numbers are not detected. The documentation of each feature lists the exact fields that leave your account: error tracking, uptime checks, alarms, security scanning and incident AI.

Credentials you give the Console, such as Git and Slack connections, single sign-on client secrets and AI API keys, are stored encrypted.

3. What stays in your AWS account

  • Application logs. Error tracking sends only the error details listed above.
  • Build artifacts and deployment packages, in your stack's deployment bucket.
  • Package lists. To check them for vulnerabilities, the Console reads them in memory only and sends each package's name and version to OSV.dev.
  • Secrets and parameters.
  • Synthetic test results, screenshots and logs.
  • Tokens, webhook URLs and secrets of the alert channels set in your configuration (Slack, Microsoft Teams, Discord, email and webhooks).
  • Your infrastructure and the data in it, such as databases and buckets.

The Console acts in your AWS account through the IAM role that the connection creates, with temporary credentials. Stacktape does not store long-lived AWS access keys. AWS CloudTrail in your account records what the role does. You can remove the access at any time by deleting the connection.

4. Data sent to other services

Some features send data to other services, only when you use them:

  • OSV.dev: the name and version of each package in your stack's package list, to find known vulnerabilities.
  • Your AI provider (Anthropic, OpenAI or xAI): when your organization connects its own API key, a scrubbed, bounded snapshot of what Stacktape stores about an incident, so the provider can write the incident's briefing. The provider processes it under your agreement with them. Investigate and Fix runs execute on your project's runner in your AWS account. The coding agent you choose sends what it reads to its provider, under your organization's key or your own subscription.
  • GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket and Slack: when you connect them, the Console works with the repositories you connect, for example to deploy on push or to open a draft pull request, and posts alerts to the Slack channels you choose.

5. Providers we use

  • Amazon Web Services hosts the Console and its database in eu-west-1 (Ireland), hosts this website and sends our emails.
  • PostHog, in the EU, receives CLI telemetry and crash reports, Console usage analytics and error reports, and documentation analytics.
  • Plausible Analytics counts visits to stacktape.com.
  • Paddle processes payments and issues invoices for paid plans.
  • Apollo.io receives your name and email address when you sign up for the Console yourself, not by invitation. It looks up professional information about you, such as your company and its website, and sends you a short series of emails from us that offer help with your first deploy.
  • Google and GitHub sign you in, if you choose to sign in with them.
  • Slack carries our team's internal notifications, such as a new sign-up with the person's name and email, or a customer's first deploy. It also hosts the shared channels we have with customers.
  • Cal.com handles booking a call with us.

Each provider handles data under its own terms and privacy policy, and Stacktape is not responsible for how they do so. Stacktape may also give information to authorities and law enforcement when the law requires it.

6. This website, the documentation and cookies

stacktape.com uses Plausible Analytics, which counts visits without cookies. The documentation at docs.stacktape.com uses PostHog without cookies or any identifier stored in your browser, and records page views, time on page and script errors. Neither site uses session recording.

The Console uses cookies to keep you signed in. Its usage analytics store an ID in a cookie and in your browser's local storage.

7. How we use your data

We use the data described here to run the Console and its features, to bill paid plans, and to find and fix what fails in Stacktape. We use your email address to:

  • answer your questions and requests;
  • send the alerts and notifications you set up, and messages about your account, organization and plan;
  • tell you about changes to Stacktape, this policy and the terms of use;
  • send our newsletter and other emails about Stacktape, which you can unsubscribe from at any time.

8. How long we keep data

  • We keep Console data while your account and organization exist.
  • Audit log events are kept for 400 days, also after the organization is deleted.
  • Error tracking keeps the newest 100 occurrence samples of each issue.
  • After your account is closed, we may keep data for as long as we have a legitimate reason to. We may also give it to law enforcement or other authorities entitled to it, even after the account is closed.

9. Security

Data sent to and from Stacktape is encrypted in transit with TLS, and the Console's database is encrypted at rest.

10. Your rights

You can ask to see the personal data we hold about you, and to have it corrected, updated or deleted. This includes deleting your Console account, which is not self-service. Email info@stacktape.com from the address you use with Stacktape. A request can take up to 14 days. To protect you, we may ask you to prove your identity, and we can decline the request if you cannot.

To stop CLI telemetry, set STP_DISABLE_TELEMETRY=1 or DO_NOT_TRACK=1.

11. Changes to this policy

We may change this policy at any time. Changes do not apply retroactively. We tell registered users and newsletter subscribers at least seven days before a new policy takes effect. Changes that address urgent matters or are required by law can take effect immediately, and we tell you about them too. If you do not agree with a change, stop using Stacktape's services.
